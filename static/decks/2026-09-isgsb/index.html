What are the techniques, that we talk about today, actually useful for? What problem are we trying to solve? In general, NDEs and UDEs can fit "arbitrary" time series. For us however, the most useful approach is to use them to try to understand the unknown parts of the system. So to extend a known ODE model in order to be able to fit experimental data. As an illustrative example, here we have a PAM chlorophyll fluorescence trace, where the blue ODE gets the shape roughly right but visibly misses the fine structure. If we now extend our ODE with a learned component, we can get the green UDE curve which fits the data really well. As a potential further option, we could now for example perform symbolic regression on the learned network term to recover a concrete equation, but that's beyond the topic of today. Today we will just talk about how we can get to that green curve in the first place.

Now that you understand their use case, let's define what UDEs and NDEs actually are. To set up some notation before we get into that, let's take a simple linear chain and write it as a system of ODEs.

Then in order to to expand on the mechanism, we simply bolt on a small fully connected network. The left part is the ODE, if we just had the right part we would call this an NDE or NODE and together we call this a UDE. And that's already the entire trick, nothing fancy happening here. The complexity will start creeping in once we actually want to train all of the parameters in the model, but that already handled for you by the respective packages you may use. As a minor gripe: technically all of this is still a normal system of ODEs. The different terminology is mostly to communicate the intent behind choosing the different approaches.

Let's go through a real world example together. Here we worked together with Lisa Fürtauer's lab to try to understand plant sugar metabolism in cold-night stress. So we had time-resolved sugar concentrations of plants grown through a cycle of 12 hour nights at 18 deg C and 12 hour days at 22 deg, which is indicated with the shading here. Then for the stress condition the plants were put into 4 deg C during the nights. CND1 refers to the first day of the stress and CND7 to the seventh day. The goal was here to identify the fluxes of key enzymes over time and how they changed in those stress conditions.

In this case we already had an ODE model that Lisa's group developed for prior work, but we couldn't get it to fit the data with classical approaches no matter what we tried. So we decided to give UDEs a shot and quite quickly had reasonable results. Specifically, what we did here was to only learn the flux vector v and to re-use the stoichiometric matrix we had from the old model. That way, we could still assign each model output to an actual flux - even though we don't know the exact rate equation for that flux anymore. In the end, we did fit an ensemble of 100 models per condition, such that we could also approximate the uncertainty behind our predictions.

Since these kinds of models make it comparatively easy to come up with a predictive model and you can AI codegen an analysis in a matter of minutes nowadays, I really want to make one thing clear though: the fact that you have a good fit, does not necessarily mean that you identified the correct mechanism. If you forget about the entirety of this talk except for this one sentence I'm still going home a happy man, so it's worth repeating: a good fit, and even a predictive model does not mean that you identified the correct mechanism. There are a couple of reasons why that might be, such as parameter multiplicity, an confounded underlying causal model or simply that your validation dataset shared too much structure with the training set. So I believe it's really important in our communication to clearly state that such results are a suggestion at best. This was always a problem by the way. Most ODE models in biology have issues with identifiability and so on, but now people with less background in modeling can build these and they might not have enough appreciation for this fact. So please, both in communicating with other people as well as when writing papers about your new methods, try not to oversell this point.

Now that we know what UDEs are, what they can and can't do, let's talk about the fun part that usually doesn't make it into papers: that is, what are all the problem you'll come accross when you actually try to do this yourself? Just imagine, plugging an arbitrary function with randomly initialised parameters into an ODE solver. That already sounds like asking for trouble. Now imagine, you actually try to train this function over a large parameter space. What could go wrong? In my experience so far, you will sooner or later hit some problems. Your problem could get stiff, the integration fails for numerous reaons or the gradient information you need to fit the model vanishes or explodes. Luckily for us, most of these can be tamed.

Here are some solutions that I found in general work quite well. No single one will be sufficient on their own, but if you stack multiple of them you can usually get quite far. In general, it's a good idea to do some form of curriculum learning - so you first train your model on shorter snippets of your data and over time expand these to longer and longer subsets. Next, especially for early training, it's a good idea to first scale down the vector field of the NDE, such that it doesn't immediately cause numerical issues. You can the either make that scale a learnable parameter as well or allow your final activation to be larger than 1. To avoid jumping into overly stiff territory it's usually a good idea to keep the learning rate low, such as ten to the power of minus 4 or minus 5. This will usually mean your training takes a little more time, but avoids these regions of instabilities. In the same vein, you can reduce the momentum if you are using a momentum based optimizer and clip gradients to avoid larger steps. For the activation functions of the neural network I'd recommend smooth ones like softplus and avoiding kinked ones like ReLU. It's also generally a good idea to tighten solver tolerances, which again helps stabilising the gradient. As somewhat of a last resort when your model tends to produce very large vector fields, you might look into penalising the norm of it as an extension of your loss function. If you have additional biological information you want to use to put a soft constraint on the model, that would also be the place where to do it.

You do this when you suspect there are additional influences on your system of study, i.e. when your system isn't Markov. That is the case more often than not, as in biology we like to pretend that our system or pathway is more or less isolated. When in reality there are always some other influences that we either ignore or don't know about yet. You can model these hidden variables as well by projecting your system up into a higher dimension, solving the problem there and then projecting down again.

Let's look at a couple of ways of projecting up, which means we need to create initial conditions for the hidden variables. As a first option you can seed the hidden variables with some function of your choice from the other initial conditions. Next, you can leave them completely free or unconstrained. For example, you might make them learnable parameters or create randomly. Lastly, and most fun, is to also allow that transformation to change the other initial conditions as well. Usually, when we think about intial value problems we assume that the initial state is well known, when in reality they might contain a similar measurement error as our remaining data points and shouldn't get that special treatment. This formulation allows us to encode such behavior. I'd usually start with the left version though, which is cheap and well behaved.

On the way down we then of course do the opposite. The most forward approach here would be to simply isolate the actual variables that we measured and drop the latent or hidden dimensions. I refer to this as a hard projection. Another option is to allow the latent state to mix with the other state, again with an arbitrary function. Here you have to be careful though, as this means you can't directly read out any fluxes anymore. The only reason I'd ever do this when there are issues of propagation the gradient information through the entire system. In that case, I'd usually start with a mixture of the two and then anneal out the soft projection, so I end up with an interpretable projection in the end.

If you are now excited by this method and want to try it out yourself, as far as I know there are two major ecosystem to do that. In Python, I would suggest going with the jax ecosystem, where you can use diffrax to solve the equations and for example equinox to create your neural networks. And, of course, you can use our own mxlpy to have easy builders for such models, that take away quite a bit of the hassle of writing such models by hand. In Julia, you have the SciML ecosystem that is build for specifically these kind of problems, and there are tons of packages and resources, for example the excellent sciml lecture series. I'd recommend you to try both, then continue with the one you liked better and enjoy playing around with these models. The approach is what matters, not the langauge.

And finally: remember that a good fit is just a hypothesis, not a proof.
